Design notes

Untethered from Earth, never without guidance.

How the system is put together: one pipeline that turns any health signal into a coordinated, verified response on board, and keeps Earth informed across delays and outages. The browser prototype implements this design end to end; crew readings in it are simulated, and NASA measurements drive the environment where they exist.

01Goals and constraints

  • On board first. Advice from Earth can be minutes late or missing, so detection, decisions and confirmation happen on the ship.
  • Done means recovered. A command that reports success, or a task someone ticked, does not close a problem; only data showing recovery does.
  • Trust is earned by the data. Every reading carries its source, quality and age; unknown is never treated as normal.
  • People decide. The software is decision support. The crew medical officer makes medical decisions; the software records them and their reasons.
  • Traceable. Every threshold and procedure step names its source, or is marked as an assumption.

02Architecture

Sensors & crew reports→Data quality & statistics→Rules → events→Prediction & self-checks→Response plan per crew→Devices & alarms↔Earth link
  • One shared state, one writer per part. Each module owns its own slice of the state and reads the others; modules talk only through that state and a small set of operations. Six parts were built in parallel against shared interface contracts.
  • A test bench, kept apart from the product. A simulation of the crew, the cabin, equipment faults and the communication link stands in for missing hardware. The monitoring and response software sees only sensor readings and crew answers, never the simulation's ground truth; tests enforce this.
  • Deterministic time. The whole system advances in 10-second steps; a 32-hour mission replays identically every time, in the browser or without graphics for automated checks.
  • Configuration, not code. Sensors, rules, procedures, self-check questions and missions are data files; the same engine runs any mission described that way.

04Monitoring

  • Quality gate. Poor sensor contact, stuck values, dropouts, implausible values and conflicting sources are flagged; a doubtful reading triggers a re-measurement and never counts as healthy.
  • Statistics that match the standards. Time-weighted averages over 1 hour to 7 days, personal exposure built from where each person has been, cumulative radiation dose, and personal baselines.
  • Rules as data. Conditions such as “1-hour CO₂ above 3 mmHg” (NASA-STD-3001) are written in data files with their source; four severity levels from advisory to emergency.
  • Events with a lifecycle. Opened, escalated, cleared and closed, with versions, so later steps and messages know exactly which facts they act on.

05Prediction

  • Environment. Cabin CO₂ is forecast hours ahead; a model learned from NASA ISS cabin telemetry runs alongside a trend forecast.
  • Health. Exposure (CO₂, radiation, lost sleep) combined with each person's baseline and published exposure–response evidence predicts which indicators are likely to change and roughly when.
  • Prepare in advance. Each person's self-check plan and alerts adapt to the forecast, within a daily limit. Predictions only add checks; they never cancel an alarm.
  • Honest scoring. Every forecast is later marked as a hit or a false alarm, so the crew can judge how far to trust it.

06Response and crew coordination

  • Procedures, not improvisation. Each problem opens a written procedure whose steps say what to do, who should do it, how long it takes, whether it is hazardous, what to do if Earth cannot be reached, and how the data confirms it.
  • One plan for the whole crew. Steps from all open problems are merged, ordered (protect people, check, fix the source, treat, measure again) and assigned by role, skill, location and health.
  • Safety by design. Someone who is unwell is relieved of risky work, with the reason recorded; crewmates check on each other; hazardous work requires a watcher before the command is sent.
  • Priorities change. A more urgent event pauses lower-priority work and resumes it automatically afterwards.
  • Closing. A case closes only when its data conditions hold; health cases are signed off by the crew medical officer.

07Communication with Earth

  • Delay and outages are part of the model. Messages travel with a one-way delay; during an outage they queue and are sent when the link returns.
  • Every message knows its moment. It carries the time of the data it describes and the versions of the problems it refers to.
  • Late advice is weighed, not obeyed. When a reply arrives after the situation has changed, it is flagged as stale; the medical officer adopts or declines it with a reason, and the decision goes back to Earth.
  • Daily report. A summary of the day's events, cases and checks is sent to the ground.

08Devices, alarms and privacy

  • Wrist for alerts that find the person; tablet for each person's readings, body map, checklist and self-checks; module workstations for the cabin's caution and warning, environment, events and crew status; a ground console for the flight surgeon.
  • Graded alarms. Four levels, each with its own colour and sound pattern, following the caution-and-warning conventions of NASA-STD-3001 Volume 2.
  • Right audience. Ship-wide hazards are shared with everyone; personal health alerts go to the person and the medical officer, while others see only “Crew health – name”.

09Missions and outposts

A mission is a package of sensor registry, rules, procedures, self-check questions and scenario. Two packages run on the same engine:

  • Mars-transit spacecraft — four crew, three modules and a shelter, Earth minutes away and sometimes out of contact.
  • Mars surface — a base and a pressurized rover, the crew split two and two; each side keeps working on its own data when the relay drops; alarms move to the helmet during a spacewalk; procedures scale to a two-person crew with medical support from the base.

11Verification

  • Scenario checks. Each mission scenario is replayed without graphics and checked against written expectations (for example: a poor-contact reading must not raise an alarm; a successful restart must not close the CO₂ case; late advice must be flagged).
  • Automated tests across every module, including tests that make sure no device or monitor can read the simulation's ground truth.
  • Repeatable. Deterministic time and seeded noise give the same result on every run.

12Extending the system

  • New sensors and devices — an EEG headband, a glucose meter, real wearables — join as registry entries, rules and procedures.
  • New outposts — a lunar base, a station — are new mission packages on the same engine.
  • An evidence assistant could explain readings from reviewed sources, while rules and people stay in control of actions.
  • Review of thresholds and procedures with flight medicine experts, and testing with real hardware.
